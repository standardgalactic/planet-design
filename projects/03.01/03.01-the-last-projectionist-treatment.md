# The Last Projectionist: Treatment

Catalogue slot 03.01 | Cycle: Work and Its Afterlives | Framework: inverse problems, underdetermination | Scale M

## Logline

Nine days before a condemned cinema closes, its head projectionist finds handwritten corrections on old prints that seem to document a scene audiences remember but the films no longer contain. He builds a coherent account of what was removed and why, accuses the wrong man with it, and learns that the evidence he needed had been in his own booth the whole time.

## Framework and conditions

The story dramatizes an inverse problem: the surviving observations are fixed, many histories could have produced them, and each new observation removes some candidate histories while admitting others.

- The fiction states explicitly which observations each character holds and which hypotheses each observation eliminates.
- A reconstruction that fits all current evidence is not thereby the true history. The film does not claim that evidence alone always suffices; here it finally does, because one overlooked record discriminates between the survivors.
- Silence is treated as a missing observation, not as a finding.

## Setting and deadline

The Meridian Cinema, a single-screen theatre in a lakeside town, closes after a final screening on the ninth evening. A demolition survey requires every print and record to be inventoried before then. Three recurring locations carry the whole film:

- **The auditorium.** 412 seats, a proscenium screen, a house with sightlines that change the story depending on where a character sat in 1987.
- **The projection booth.** Two projectors, a rewind bench, shelves of cans, and a paper logbook kept by every projectionist since the building opened.
- **Pell's Café.** Next door, with a back room that once served as the theatre's overflow lobby and a cellar that shares a wall with the Meridian's old storeroom.

## Principal characters

**Aurel Dacey, 63, head projectionist.** Thirty-eight years in the booth, arriving in 1988, the season after the disputed festival, and inheriting its logbook. Trusts splices, counts, and cue marks over people. Believes that any gap in a record is a place where someone intervened.

**Margo Pell, 74, owner of Pell's Café.** Former box-office cashier. Warm, evasive about 1987, and the keeper of a promise the story reveals late.

**Benedikt Lowe, 71, retired schoolteacher.** A regular since childhood. Remembers a scene vividly (a red van leaving by the lake road, with a particular piece of music) and has told that memory as fact for thirty years.

**Priya Anand, 29, municipal heritage assessor.** Outsider conducting the closure inventory. Counts, measures, and photographs without sentiment. Notices the labels on the cans.

**Tobias Voss, 58, director of the family company that owns the building.** In 1987 he was nineteen, an usher alongside Daniel Wray. His family's red van is the object at the center of the town's story. Appears to be closing the theatre to bury the past.

Absent figures: **Edmund Hale** (projectionist until 1999, deceased) and **Daniel Wray** (usher, nineteen, who vanished the night of the 1987 harvest festival screening).

## Ground truth (private development ledger)

This is what actually happened. It is never stated in one place on the page; readers assemble it.

1. In autumn 1987 the regional censorship board ordered 41 feet deleted from the release print of *The Salt Orchard*, a scene in which the heroine leaves in a red van by the lake road. The lab applied a label to each can, "REGIONAL BOARD DELETIONS: 41 FT," before delivery to the Meridian. The Meridian's print never contained the scene.
2. Daniel Wray, an aspiring filmmaker, obtained the deleted footage from a donated reel. With Edmund Hale's help he spliced it into a one-night insert for the harvest festival screening, intending it as a restoration of the film he loved.
3. Edmund pencilled working notes on the leaders of three prints, including "H.V. INS after cue 7, OUT after" and the footage count. "H.V." means Harvest Viewing. Daniel had begun restoring cut scenes from two other films in the same way, and his handwriting appears beside Edmund's on those leaders.
4. On the festival night the insert was projected once. The audience saw a scene the film did not contain, and the booth logbook records two changeovers more than the reel count requires, inside the festival's scheduled nine-minute intermission. The insert itself ran about 27 seconds (41 ft of 35 mm at 24 fps). The log notes that the house lights were lowered again for a supplementary projection; the return to the feature is the second extra changeover. The footage shows an identifiable stretch of the lake road, which ties the red van to the Voss family's vehicle.
5. Daniel's father was violent, and Daniel could not return home after that night. Tobias drove the family's red van to the lake-road bus stop at 9:40 so Daniel could leave for film school. Margo knew, and received one letter from Daniel with the original insert reel. Daniel asked both of them for silence. He died in 2009.
6. The town searched the lake. Tobias and Margo kept the promise because breaking it would have exposed Daniel's father's household, and by the time that father died the silence had become habit.

## Evidence ledger and hypotheses

Candidate histories of the missing scene:

- **H1 Cover-up.** The scene was in the release print and was removed on Harlan Voss's orders to hide the red van.
- **H2 Conflated memory.** No such scene was ever shown; patrons merged it with another film.
- **H3 Source censorship.** The scene was deleted before the print reached the Meridian; patrons recall trailers or press material.
- **H4 One-night insert.** A separate reel was shown once, assembled from the deleted footage.
- **H5 Physical loss.** The scene was destroyed by damage and the original screening is what patrons remember.

### Two separate inverse problems

The screening history and Daniel's departure are different problems with different evidence. Establishing a one-night insert does not show Daniel left voluntarily, and a disappearance does not show the film was altered to conceal it. Aurel's central error is binding them into one explanation.

Candidate accounts of the departure:

- **D1** Daniel left voluntarily, with help.
- **D2** Daniel was harmed by someone connected to the Voss family.
- **D3** Daniel left alone, circumstances unknown.

Tobias's absence from 9:40 to 11:15 fits all three until the letter and his own account arrive. The screening records give chronology only. Daniel's intention rests on the letter and the two participants' accounts. Aurel's correction is to separate the problems and weigh the evidence for each independently.

Two cautions on the table above. O4 narrows toward a separate projection but does not say what was projected. O3 shows the delivered print lacked the footage, subject to the label's provenance; it does not rule out a later insert and removal. The conjunction of O1 to O5, the programme expansion of "H.V." as Harvest Viewing, and the lake-road footage is what makes H4 persuasive.

## Treatment

### Act One: Days 9 to 7

Aurel prepares the Meridian for its last week. Priya arrives with a clipboard to inventory the building and the print vault. While rewinding *The Salt Orchard* he finds pencilled notes on the leader, "H.V. INS after cue 7, OUT after, 41 FT", and identical notes on two other prints, in two different hands. He reads "H.V." as Harlan Voss, the owner at the time, and "OUT" as an instruction to remove footage.

At Pell's Café he asks Benedikt about the film. Benedikt recalls, in precise detail, a scene with a red van on the lake road and a rising cello line, and the sound the audience made. He remembers it from the festival night in 1987, the night Daniel Wray disappeared. Aurel, who has never seen that scene in the print, now has two observations and a motive. He starts to rebuild the sequence from the notes and Benedikt's description: a scene shown once, then cut at Voss's order because the van was the family's.

The first reconstruction is fully coherent. It explains the notes, the memory, the van, and the disappearance. Aurel does not ask what else would explain them.

### Act Two: Days 6 to 3

Aurel pursues the account. He draws the scene on the rewind bench, uses the footage count to place it in reel four, and asks Margo and others to describe it. Each recollection is filtered through his version; Benedikt adjusts his memory toward the cut and begins adding details he had never mentioned. Tobias, who has declined to attend the final screening, is visibly shaken when Aurel raises the van.

At the midpoint, in front of Margo, Benedikt and several patrons, Aurel states his account and names Harlan Voss and by extension Tobias. The room divides. Tobias leaves, and the company threatens to cancel the final screening. Margo says nothing, which Aurel reads as agreement.

Priya, completing the inventory, photographs the cans and points out that each carries a lab label dated before delivery: REGIONAL BOARD DELETIONS, 41 FT. The print never held the scene. The cover-up account is eliminated by a stamp Aurel has handled for decades. His response is to adopt the next coherent story: the board cut the scene, and Benedikt's memory is a composite of press material. It fits the new observation, and he sets it as firmly as the first.

### Act Three: Days 2 to final night

Priya, reconciling the logbook against the print records, asks why the festival night shows two more changeovers than the reels need and a note of a supplementary projection inside the nine-minute festival intermission. The logbook is in Aurel's booth. Having inherited it in 1988, he has handled it for decades without reading that page as evidence.

The page does not say what was projected, but it records a separate projection that strains H1 and H3 and shifts weight to H4. Aurel asks Tobias to meet him, apologizes for the accusation, and says what the log shows. Aurel withdraws the accusation without substituting a new verdict. Tobias, after a long silence, tells the part he can tell: on the festival night he drove the family's red van to the lake-road bus stop with a passenger who asked him to tell no one. He does not name the passenger. Margo, who has overheard from the café, completes the account and says the name.

Margo leads Aurel and Priya to the cellar, where Daniel's one letter and the original insert reel have lain beside the storeroom wall. The letter explains the departure and the request for silence. Edmund's notes and Daniel's hand are matched at last.

On the closing evening the Meridian screens *The Salt Orchard* with the insert restored in the position the notes specify. Benedikt sees the scene again and recognizes that his thirty-year memory was of a single projection, not of the film. Tobias sits in the house for the first time in thirty-eight years. Aurel runs the changeover himself, logs it in the booth book in his own hand, and hands the reel to Priya for conservation. He switches off the lamp.

## What Aurel's error costs

- A public accusation that damages Tobias, who was protecting a friend.
- Benedikt's trust in his own memory, which Aurel's questioning had reshaped.
- Two days lost to a second coherent story built before checking the booth's own record.

What he learns is operational, not moral: a reconstruction is a set of hypotheses held against observations still to come, and the unreviewed record in his own possession is a standing candidate for the observation that decides.

## Reconstruction convention (for the visual bible)

- Observed material (prints, labels, logbook pages, objects) is rendered in the primary charcoal style with hard edges.
- Every reconstructed event, whatever its source, uses one shared marker: perforation-edged frames with a flickering inner border. The same convention applies to Aurel's first account, the second, and the account that turns out true, so the marker signals provisional status without signaling which is correct.
- Reconstructions appear only where a character is speaking or drawing them, and always from that character's evidence.
- The festival night itself is not drawn as a direct memory until the closing screening, where the audience's view of the screen replaces any reconstruction.

## Open decisions (resolved in the scene outline)

- Final cast ages and whether the 1987 sequences use flashback panels or only reconstructions.
- How much of the second hand on the leaders is Daniel's, and where the two hands first appear on the page.
- Whether Priya's inventory forms a recurring structural device (a numbered count that advances with each scene).
- Whether the closing screening is shown in full panels or by audience reaction alone.
