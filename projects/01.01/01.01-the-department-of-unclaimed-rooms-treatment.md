# The Department of Unclaimed Rooms: Treatment

Catalogue slot 01.01 | Cycle: Cities That Think | Framework: map versus territory, classification | Kind M/C | Scale M

## Logline

Thirty days before a refit of a six-storey building begins, a municipal surveyor measuring it for the final register finds five occupied rooms that appear on no current floor plan, each held by a resident with solid paper proof of living there. She restores the rooms to the map, certain that an accurate map will repair the mismatch. It repairs nothing. Each correction decides who is counted, who is rehoused, and who is quietly left outside the city's sight, and she has to learn that the register was never a copy of the building but a rule for what counts.

## Framework and conditions

The story dramatizes classification. A register is a function from physical things to categories, and every category carries rights and duties. Putting a room on the map does not copy it; it applies a rule to it.

- **The territory is not mysterious.** Every room in the building can be measured with a tape. The story does not claim that reality is unknowable. What is contested is which rooms count as dwellings, and what follows when they do.
- **Visibility depends on the scheme, not on presence.** A room that is invisible to one office is listed by another because the offices use different inclusion rules.
- **Every correction has a selection effect.** Adding to a map under a fixed budget or capacity removes something else. The fiction states each correction and who it adds and removes.
- **No register is wrong by its own rule.** The error is believing that one register ought to equal the territory.

## Setting and deadline

Calloway House, a six-storey former hotel converted in 1974 into 48 flats, is to begin a structural refit in thirty days. The refit's rehousing plan is generated from the Department of Building Registers' plan of the building. Three recurring locations carry the film:

- **The building.** A central stair, narrow corridors, a rear service strip with a light well, and doors that the plan does not draw.
- **The Register Room.** A basement office in the Department of Building Registers, flat files, plan chests and a terminal that validates every entry against the Housing Standard.
- **The caretaker's room.** Ground floor, behind the lobby: keys on boards, a ledger, a kettle, a hatch onto the street.

A thirty-day countdown runs through the book. Day 30 is the survey. Day 0 is the day the contractor takes the north stack.

## Principal characters

**Ilse Varga, 41, surveyor.** Department of Building Registers. Trusts drawn dimensions and believes an accurate drawing is a repairable one. Precise, tired, conscientious. Has never knowingly altered a record.

**Desmond Okafor, 59, Registrar.** Her superior and keeper of the classification manual. Not an obstructionist. He wants the refit to start, the grant to be spent, and the register to stay defensible. Thinks of the plan as the instrument by which the city sees.

**Ottilie Brandt, 84, resident of Room 3R.** Has lived in her room since 1982. Her papers are in a shoebox, mostly in her late husband's name. She will not sign anything that describes her room as unclassified.

**Teodor Kask, 35, courier, resident of Room 5R.** Has six independent records of his address, from the bank, library, electoral roll, a mobile account and others. Practical, wary of forms, good at paper.

**Gordon Pike, 67, caretaker.** In the building since 1979. Keeps a key ledger and a drawer of handwritten tenancy slips. Believes he did people a favour and committed an offence in the same act.

Absent figure: **E. Lindqvist**, the 1974 conversion clerk whose rule excluded the rear rooms.

## Ground truth (private development ledger)

1. In 1931 the building is a hotel with 48 guest suites and 5 small rear staff rooms, each on its own service landing (Rooms 2R to 6R, one per floor from the second floor up). Each staff room has its own electricity sub-meter.
2. In 1974 the hotel is converted into 48 flats. The conversion drawing covers only the front of the building. The rear strip is left blank, outside the scope of the works. The 1974 dwelling standard requires at least 9 square metres, a window onto a street or a court at least 3 metres wide, and a door onto a shared corridor. The rear rooms measure between 7.4 and 8.6 square metres and open onto a light well 2.4 metres wide.
3. The department's working plan is the 1974 drawing, so the rear strip does not exist on it. The 1931 hotel plans are in the heritage archive, held by a different office, and show the rooms as staff rooms.
4. Gordon Pike becomes caretaker in 1979. He starts letting the dry, furnished rear rooms to people the parish sends him. He writes a tenancy slip for each and collects rent into a cash tin. The rooms remain physically unchanged.
5. The occupants register with other offices. Electricity bills them as the occupants of the sub-meters. The electoral roll lists "Rm 3R, Calloway House". The library, a bank and a mobile account accept the room as an address. The department's register never learns of any of it, because it asks only whether a space is drawn on the plan and meets the standard.
6. Three listed flats are empty, and have been for years. One tenant died in 2021, one left in 2022 and one in 2023. Their tenancy records were never closed because closing needs a form that nobody filed. The register still counts them as occupied.
7. The refit's rehousing plan for phase one (the north stack, which includes rear rooms 4R, 5R and 6R) has 10 places, set from the register's count of 8 listed households in that stack. It was never run against the real building.

## Register visibility (the central exhibit)

Who sees what, by each office's own rule:

| Register | Rule for "a place someone lives" | The 48 listed flats | The 5 rear rooms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planning register (1974 plan) | Drawn on the plan and meets the dwelling standard | All 48 counted, including 3 empty | Not drawn |
| Heritage plans (1931) | Any room on the hotel drawings | 48 guest suites | 5 staff rooms |
| Electricity service points | Metered point with an account | 48 | 5 sub-meters, all billed |
| Electoral roll | Resident gives an address | 45 occupied flats | 5 rooms, 5 of 6 residents enrolled |
| Bank, library and mobile proofs | Accepts a stated address | Accepted | Accepted for four rooms, refused for one |

No row is an error by its own rule. The register Ilse is asked to certify is the one that sees least.

## Three-act treatment

### Act One: Days 30 to 24. A mismatch

Ilse arrives with a laser scanner and tape to produce the final survey. The volume of each floor is larger than the sum of its drawn rooms. Behind a service door on the second floor she finds a small room with a bed, a kettle, a radio and a man writing a letter. She apologizes and checks the plan; the strip is blank. On the other floors she finds four more.

Each resident shows her documents without being asked. Teodor opens a folder of six records. Ottilie brings the shoebox. Gordon Pike, called in, says "It is on my ledger" and nothing else. Desmond, shown the discrepancy, says the plan is the plan and asks her to confirm the rooms are real. She measures them and they are.

Ilse concludes the plan is wrong. In her view a wrong plan is a defect, a defect can be corrected, and a corrected plan will restore the territory. She sets out to put the rooms back.

### Act Two: Days 23 to 8. Corrections select

She pulls the 1931 drawings from the heritage archive and restores the five rooms as habitable rooms (C1). She files the correction on the system and goes home.

The next morning the validator has failed all five against the dwelling standard and generated five closing notices with 28-day periods. Teodor's courier firm checks addresses against closing notices and suspends him. The rehousing plan now shows 11 households for 10 places. The manual ranks by documented tenancy, and the lowest-ranked household is the young couple in 6C, who had never been told there was a problem.

Ilse withdraws the correction (C2). The notices are rescinded, but the rooms are back to being stores and the residents back to nothing. Desmond reminds her that rescinding is not undoing.

She tries a rule that seems fair: include only the rooms whose occupants hold proofs from at least three offices in their own names (C3). It lets in four households. It leaves out Ottilie, who has lived in her room longest, because her proofs are in her husband's name. Ottilie has already refused to sign any form that calls her room anything other than her home. When she hears what Ilse's rule did she says nothing and closes her door.

The cost reaches Ilse. She has altered the register three times in a fortnight, and each alteration was neutral by some rule and harmful by another.

### Act Three: Days 7 to 0. The records separate

Ilse stops trying to fix the plan and lays out every register beside the plan: the heritage drawings, the electricity points, the electoral roll, the proofs, Gordon's ledger and slips. She builds the visibility table. Each register sees a different building. None is mistaken by its own rule. The plan is the register that governs rehousing, and it is the one that sees least.

In the same exercise she checks occupancy in the other direction. Three listed flats are empty, held on the register by tenancy records nobody closed. The register has been counting ghosts. She now has three flats the plan counts as occupied, and five rooms the plan does not count at all.

She corrects the rehousing arithmetic on that basis: the pool for phase one is 10 places plus the 3 empty flats, which is 13 places for 11 households. The household in 6C keeps its place.

For the rear rooms she proposes not another correction but a new class in the manual: **Occupied, unclassified**. It carries the same rehousing entitlement as a classified household, requires only a physical survey and one independent record, and does not trigger the dwelling standard's closing notices. Desmond objects that a manual amendment takes six weeks and that the contractor's start date is in four days. He reads her proposal, then Gordon's ledger, then the table. He signs a temporary direction under his own authority, accepting the risk that the class could be abused by landlords to house people below standard. It is written into the direction that the class is a protection against invisibility, not an exemption from fitness.

Gordon agrees to hand over his tenancy slips as records. Ilse records them with his consent, knowing it exposes him to a fine for unlicensed letting. He is fined and keeps his job.

The refit's scope already includes widening the light well. After the works, 2R, 4R, 5R and 6R will meet the standard. Room 3R cannot, because a structural column intrudes, but under the new class Ottilie may stay in it by choice. She signs the form after Ilse changes the line that offended her, from "unclassified" to a line that records the room as measured, occupied since 1982, and held by her.

On the last morning Ilse measures Room 3R with Ottilie standing in the doorway. She writes 7.9 square metres and the date of first occupation. Gordon, who has been waiting in the corridor, screws a small brass plate to the door: 3R. It is the first number that room has ever had on the city's side of the door.

## What Ilse's error costs

- **Closing notices.** Five automatically issued, and a courier suspended for nine days.
- **A bumped household.** The couple with an infant in 6C lose their place in the first correction, and live for a week not knowing.
- **Ottilie's trust,** which is only partly restored.
- **Gordon's fine,** which is the price of his ledger becoming a record.
- **Fourteen days,** spent altering a register three times before asking what each alteration would select.

What she learns is operational. A register is a rule with consequences, and a correction is a change of rule. Before changing it, she must say what it adds, what it removes, and who it was drawn to see.

## Visual convention (for the visual bible)

- Precise building sections, orderly panels. The Graphic note in the catalogue asks for panels that grow harder to reconcile, and the treatment gives that a mechanism: each register is drawn as its own section of the same building.
- **One building, several sections.** The planning section, the heritage section, the electricity section and the caretaker's section are always drawn from the same viewpoint and at the same scale. The rear strip is hatched on one, drawn as rooms on another, and drawn as a column of meters on a third.
- **The reconciling device.** As the story proceeds, panels stack the sections on top of one another and the overlay becomes harder to read. In Act Three the overlay is rebuilt as a single section with a legend that shows which register sees each room.
- **The brass plate.** The final image is the physical plate on the physical door.

## Open decisions for the scene outline

- Whether the building is shown only through Ilse's survey drawings or also in direct photographic panels.
- Whether the thirty-day countdown is a recurring card or a line on the register terminal.
- How much of the manual's wording appears on the page.
- Whether Teodor's suspension or the infant's household is the more prominent cost in Act Two.
